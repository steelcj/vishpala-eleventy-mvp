These aren't a compliance checklist. They're the practical form of
Universal Cake — our own framework for asking, before building or
choosing anything, who it actually serves and what it costs the people
who depend on it if that relationship goes wrong. Universal Cake starts
from the most fundamental requirements and builds up: a site nobody can
read can't deliver on anything else it promises.

## Accessibility

Our first commitment, because it's the one everything else depends on.
A site that's unreadable to someone using a screen reader, unusable on
an old device, or written in a language a visitor doesn't speak, has
failed before anything else about it matters. That includes people with
vision, hearing, motor, and speech differences, and it includes economic
and cognitive accessibility — whether something is affordable, learnable
without training, and forgiving of mistakes.

## Sustainability

We try not to spend other people's bandwidth, battery, or electricity by
default. Concretely, not aspirationally: every font this site uses is
served from our own servers, not fetched from a third party's — no
request to Google Fonts or any other external service just to render
text. It's a small thing on its own, and it's the kind of small thing
that adds up across millions of page loads, and across visitors on
slower or less reliable connections than the one this was built on.

## Sovereignty

You shouldn't have to trust our word that you could leave if you needed
to — you should be able to check. Every page of this site is a plain
text file, in an open format, tracked in version control. If the tools
we build with disappeared tomorrow, the content wouldn't disappear with
them.

## Data Portability

Closely tied to sovereignty, and worth naming on its own: nothing here
is locked behind a proprietary export process. What's readable by a
program is also readable by a person — a technically complete export
that only a developer can open isn't the same guarantee as one anyone
can.

## Longevity

We can't promise any particular tool we use today will still exist in
ten years — nobody honestly can. What we can commit to is choosing
plain, open formats and widely-used, forkable tools over proprietary or
fashionable ones, specifically so that the content outlives whatever
we're currently using to build it.
